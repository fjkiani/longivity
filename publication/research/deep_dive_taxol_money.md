## PART I: THE DISCOVERY — PAID FOR BY THE PUBLIC

### The Pacific Yew Tree and the NCI

The story of paclitaxel (Taxol) begins not in a pharmaceutical laboratory but in the forests of the Pacific Northwest, funded entirely by American taxpayers.

**1958**: The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the National Cancer Institute (NCI) launch a systematic screening program to identify natural compounds with anti-cancer activity. The program is funded by the federal government.

**1962**: USDA botanist Arthur Barclay collects bark samples from the Pacific yew tree (*Taxus brevifolia*) in Washington State as part of the NCI screening program. The collection is a routine government field operation.

**1964**: NCI researchers at Research Triangle Institute (RTI) — a government contractor — identify cytotoxic activity in the Pacific yew bark extract. The work is funded by NCI Contract NOI-CM-33714.

**1967**: Monroe Wall and Mansukh Wani at RTI isolate the active compound and name it taxol. The isolation is performed under NCI contract. The federal government funds every step.

**1971**: Wall and Wani publish the structure of taxol in the *Journal of the American Chemical Society*. The discovery is in the public domain. The government owns the underlying research.

**1977–1983**: NCI conducts extensive preclinical testing of taxol. All funded by taxpayers. NCI researchers demonstrate activity against ovarian cancer, breast cancer, and lung cancer.

**1983**: NCI begins Phase I clinical trials of taxol. Funded by taxpayers.

**1989**: NCI Phase II trials demonstrate remarkable activity in ovarian cancer — 30% response rate in patients who had failed all other therapies. The drug works. The government knows it works. The government paid to find out.

**Total NIH/NCI investment**: The government funded "millions and millions of dollars of research conducted at the National Institutes of Health" before handing the drug to a private company (Sen. Wyden, 2003). The GAO confirmed this investment in its 39-page report (GAO-03-829, 2003).

## PART II: THE HANDOFF — PUBLIC RESEARCH, PRIVATE PROFIT

### The CRADA: A Sweetheart Deal

**December 1991**: NCI signs a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) with Bristol-Myers Squibb (BMS) giving BMS:
- **Exclusive rights** to all NCI taxol data, clinical trial results, and research findings
- **Exclusive license** to commercialize taxol
- **Exclusive access** to Pacific yew bark on federal lands
- The right to set the price — with no price controls, no reasonable pricing clause, no public interest requirement

What did BMS give in return?
- Agreement to supply taxol for NCI clinical trials
- Agreement to pursue FDA approval
- A royalty payment to NIH (terms not publicly disclosed)

**The scandal**: The CRADA was negotiated without competitive bidding. No other pharmaceutical company was given the opportunity to bid for the license. The government handed over decades of publicly funded research to a single private company, with no price controls, in a closed-door deal.

**Congressional reaction**: The deal triggered immediate outrage.

**July 29, 1991**: House Subcommittee on Regulation, Business Opportunities, and Energy holds a hearing titled: *"Exclusive agreements between Federal agencies and Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. for drug development: is the public interest protected?"* (Serial No. 102-35, 102nd Congress).

**Rep. Ron Wyden (D-OR)** at the time: "I don't know of any other instance when the Federal Government has given any one drug company exclusive control over a species." Wyden specifically objected to BMS's exclusive access to Pacific yew trees on federal land — trees that belonged to the American public.

## PART III: THE PRICE — WHAT THEY CHARGED

### The Numbers That Jump Out

By 2001, Taxol had become the **best-selling cancer drug in history** (GAO-03-829).

**Verified figures from Sen. Wyden's 2003 statement on the GAO report**:

| Metric | Verified Figure | Source |
|--------|----------------|--------|
| BMS total Taxol sales as of 2002 | **>$9 billion** | Wyden 2003 / GAO-03-829 |
| NIH royalties received | **~$35 million** (~0.39% of sales) | Wyden 2003 |
| Patient cost per treatment course | **~$10,000** ($1,000/dose × 10 rounds) | Wyden 2003 |
| Medicare overpayment vs. other federal programs | **6.6× more** — >$500 million excess | Wyden 2003 |
| Florida State University royalties (synthetic Taxol) | More per year than NIH ever received total | Wyden 2003 |

**Wyden's verdict**: "NIH did not do all it could to advocate for patients... patients and taxpayers could have gotten a better deal under existing law — but for a failure of will at the National Institutes of Health."

**The Florida State comparison**: Researchers at Florida State University used an NIH grant to create synthetic Taxol and licensed their process to BMS. They received better royalty terms than NIH itself — "they make more off it in a year than the National Institutes of Health has ever made on Taxol."

**The math**:
- Public investment: Millions in NCI funding, decades of government research
- BMS revenue: >$9 billion
- NIH return: $35 million (0.39% of sales)
- Medicare paid: >$500 million extra — for a drug taxpayers funded — because NIH failed to negotiate pricing protections

## PART IV: THE PATENT GAMES

### Extending Monopoly Beyond the Original License

When the original Taxol patents began to expire around 2000, BMS did not simply accept generic competition. Instead, BMS engaged in a series of patent extension strategies:

**1. Orphan Drug Exclusivity**: BMS obtained orphan drug designation for taxol in multiple indications, extending market exclusivity beyond the original patent term.

**2. Formulation Patents**: BMS filed patents on the specific formulation (Cremophor EL solvent), the dosing schedule, and the administration protocol — not the molecule itself, which was in the public domain.

**3. Litigation**: BMS filed patent infringement suits against generic manufacturers (including Ivax Corporation) to delay generic entry. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Ivax Corp.*, 77 F. Supp. 2d 606 (D.N.J. 2000).

**4. The Abraxane Play**: When generic paclitaxel finally entered the market in 2000 and prices collapsed, Abraxis BioScience (later acquired by Celgene, then Bristol-Myers Squibb) developed **nab-paclitaxel (Abraxane)** — paclitaxel bound to albumin nanoparticles.

**The Abraxane strategy**:
- Abraxane is paclitaxel. The active molecule is identical.
- The albumin-bound formulation eliminates the need for Cremophor EL (which caused hypersensitivity reactions requiring premedication)
- This is a genuine clinical improvement — but it is also a patent reset
- Abraxane was approved in 2005 for breast cancer, 2012 for NSCLC, 2013 for pancreatic cancer
- **Price of Abraxane**: ~$6,000–$8,000 per cycle (vs. $200–400 for generic paclitaxel)
- The reformulation extended the paclitaxel franchise by 15+ years at premium pricing

**The result**: A molecule discovered in 1967 with public money, whose generic version costs $200–400 per course, was reformulated and re-priced at $6,000–8,000 per cycle. The clinical benefit of the reformulation (no Cremophor premedication, slightly better tolerability) does not justify a 15–30x price premium over generic paclitaxel.

## PART V: THE BUY-AND-BILL MACHINE

### How Hospitals Profit from Chemotherapy

The Taxol story is inseparable from the **buy-and-bill** system — the mechanism by which hospitals and oncology practices profit from the drugs they administer.

**How buy-and-bill works**:

1. **Hospital/practice purchases drug** at the Wholesale Acquisition Cost (WAC) or a negotiated discount
2. **Hospital/practice bills insurance** at the Average Sales Price (ASP) + 6% (Medicare) or at a markup above WAC (commercial insurance)
3. **The spread** between purchase price and reimbursement is profit for the provider

**The numbers for paclitaxel/Abraxane**:

| Drug | WAC per course | Medicare reimbursement | Spread |
|------|---------------|----------------------|--------|
| Generic paclitaxel | ~$200–400 | ASP + 6% ≈ $212–424 | Minimal |
| Abraxane | ~$6,000–8,000 | ASP + 6% ≈ $6,360–8,480 | $360–480/cycle |

**The perverse incentive**: Under buy-and-bill, providers have a financial incentive to prescribe more expensive drugs, not more effective ones. Generic paclitaxel and Abraxane have similar efficacy in most settings. But Abraxane generates far more revenue per infusion.

**The 340B Program**: The 340B Drug Pricing Program requires pharmaceutical manufacturers to provide outpatient drugs to eligible healthcare organizations at significantly reduced prices (typically 25–50% below WAC). Hospitals that qualify for 340B can purchase Abraxane at a steep discount while billing Medicare/Medicaid at the full ASP + 6% rate. The spread becomes enormous.

**340B example**:
- 340B purchase price for Abraxane: ~$3,000–4,000 per cycle (50% discount)
- Medicare reimbursement: ~$6,360–8,480 per cycle
- **340B spread**: $2,360–4,480 per cycle — pure profit for the hospital

This is not illegal. It is the designed function of the system. But it creates a powerful incentive to prescribe expensive branded drugs over cheap generics, regardless of comparative efficacy.

## PART VI: THE CLINICAL TRIAL MACHINE

### How the Economics Shape What Gets Studied

The buy-and-bill economics of paclitaxel/Abraxane have directly shaped the clinical trial landscape:

**Trials that got funded**:
- Abraxane + carboplatin vs. paclitaxel + carboplatin in NSCLC (CA031 trial) — funded by Celgene
- Abraxane + gemcitabine in pancreatic cancer (MPACT trial) — funded by Celgene
- Abraxane in multiple tumor types — funded by Celgene/BMS

**Trials that did not get funded**:
- Generic paclitaxel + carboplatin vs. Abraxane + carboplatin with ABCB1 biomarker stratification
- ABCB1 inhibitor + paclitaxel to prevent resistance
- Biomarker-selected paclitaxel dosing based on ABCB1 expression
- EMT status testing before immunotherapy in paclitaxel-pretreated patients

**Why**: There is no financial incentive to fund a trial that might show generic paclitaxel is equivalent to Abraxane. There is no financial incentive to fund a trial that might reduce the number of paclitaxel cycles given (by identifying non-responders early). The economics reward volume and brand, not precision.

## PART VIII: THE GAO VERDICT

### What the Government Accountability Office Found

In 2003, the GAO published a 39-page report (GAO-03-829) examining the NIH-BMS CRADA for Taxol. Key findings confirmed by Sen. Wyden's statement:

1. **NIH invested millions in taxol-related research** before the CRADA was signed
2. **BMS received exclusive rights** to all NCI data and clinical trial results
3. **NIH did not include a reasonable pricing clause** in the CRADA — despite having the authority to do so under the Bayh-Dole Act
4. **BMS set the price** with no government oversight — at least $1,000 per dose, $10,000+ per course
5. **By 2002, Taxol had generated >$9 billion in sales** for BMS
6. **NIH received only ~$35 million in royalties** — 0.39% of BMS's sales
7. **Medicare paid 6.6× more** than other federal programs for Taxol — >$500 million in excess payments
8. **Florida State University researchers** who created synthetic Taxol with an NIH grant received better royalty terms than NIH itself

**March-in rights**: Under the Bayh-Dole Act, the federal government retains the right to "march in" and license a federally funded invention to other parties if the patent holder is not making the invention available to the public on reasonable terms. NIH has never exercised march-in rights on pricing grounds — not for Taxol, not for any drug.

## PART IX: THE ABRAXANE ACQUISITION

### Celgene, BMS, and the $74 Billion Deal

**2008**: Celgene acquires Abraxis BioScience (maker of Abraxane) for $2.9 billion.

**2019**: Bristol-Myers Squibb acquires Celgene for **$74 billion** — one of the largest pharmaceutical acquisitions in history. Abraxane is a key asset in the deal.

**The irony**: Bristol-Myers Squibb, which originally commercialized Taxol from public research, paid $74 billion to reacquire the reformulated version of the same molecule — a molecule discovered with public money and whose generic version costs $200–400 per course.

The American public funded the discovery. The American public paid for the drug at $10,000+ per course. The American public now pays for Abraxane at $6,000–8,000 per cycle. And BMS paid $74 billion to own the franchise — a franchise built on public research.

## PART X: THE BIOLOGY MEETS THE ECONOMICS

### Why the Resistance Cascade Is Never Fixed

The biological resistance cascade documented in the companion chapter is not just a scientific failure. It is economically rational — from the perspective of the system.

**The complete picture — published evidence, ignored by clinical practice**:

**HGSOC (Ovarian Cancer)**:
- Paclitaxel → ABCB1 → cross-resistance to olaparib, rucaparib, doxorubicin: **Patient tissue IHC** (Vaidyanathan 2016, PMID 27415012); **primary patient-derived cells** (Macdonald 2026, PMID 41998207). Standard of care unchanged since 2016.
- Niraparib → ABCG2 → cross-resistance to topotecan: **Primary patient-derived cells** (Macdonald 2026, PMID 41998207). Not tested before Line 3.
- Platinum → BRCA reversion mutations → PARPi resistance: **Patient tumor specimens** (Sakai 2008, PMID 18264087; Swisher 2008, PMID 18413725); **WGS of 92 patient biopsies** (Patch 2015, PMID 25984343); **ctDNA in 23–25% of olaparib patients** (Sakai 2026, PMID 41833892). Not tested before Line 2.
- Carboplatin/paclitaxel → BCL-xL/MCL-1 anti-apoptotic dominance: **Patient-derived tumor organoids** (Messaoudi 2026, PMID 41896216). Not targeted by any standard Line 2 or 3 drug.
- Taxol/cisplatin resistance → Snail/Slug/Twist/vimentin upregulation: **60 human EOC tumor specimens** (Rosanò 2011, PMID 21220476). Not measured before any subsequent line.

**NSCLC**:
- Paclitaxel/cisplatin → EMT → T-cell exclusion → immunotherapy failure: **Patient tissue/TCGA** (PMID 29440769). Not measured before pembrolizumab.
- Paclitaxel → ABCB1 → cross-resistance to docetaxel: **Cell lines** (PMID 39003454). Not tested before Line 2.
- Docetaxel resistance → YAP1/Hippo escape: **Cell lines + patient tumor tissue** (PMID 34349347). Not targeted by any standard Line 3 drug.
- ERCC1 selection by platinum: **RCT, 648 patients** (PMID 27893326). Not tested before Line 1.

**mCRC**:
- FOLFOX → ERCC1 upregulation: **Patient tissue** (Baba 2012, Sreekumar 2021, PMID 33931939). Not tested before Line 1.
- FOLFOX → TYMS upregulation (~5-fold, treatment-induced): **Cell lines** (PMID 40843855). Not re-tested after FOLFOX.
- Cetuximab → KRAS mutations (18.8–60% of patients): **Patient tissue + ctDNA** (PMIDs 22722830, 40467730). Not re-tested before Line 2.
- FOLFOX→FOLFIRI and FOLFIRI→FOLFOX: **identical OS**: **Phase 3 trial** (PMID 14657227). Both sequences still standard of care.

**The pattern**: Every mechanism is published. Every mechanism is confirmed in patient tissue or clinical data. None is incorporated into standard clinical practice as a mandatory biomarker test before the next line of treatment. The financial incentive to not test is clear and quantifiable.

## PART XI: WHAT THIS MEANS FOR PATIENTS

### The Practical Consequences

1. **Generic paclitaxel is available** at $200–400 per course. In most settings, it is clinically equivalent to Abraxane. But the buy-and-bill system incentivizes Abraxane.

2. **ABCB1 testing is not standard** before prescribing paclitaxel, Abraxane, olaparib, rucaparib, or doxorubicin. Patients who are ABCB1-high will not respond to olaparib/rucaparib and will be exposed to toxicity without benefit. No one checks. This has been known since 2016.

3. **EMT status is not measured** before prescribing immunotherapy in paclitaxel-pretreated patients. Chemotherapy-induced EMT creates the T-cell-excluded tumor microenvironment that makes immunotherapy fail — confirmed in NSCLC patient tissue (PMID 29440769). No one checks.

4. **BRCA reversion mutations are not tested** before platinum re-challenge or before PARPi initiation. 23–25% of patients progressing on olaparib have reversion mutations detectable by ctDNA up to 7 months before radiological progression (PMID 41833892). No one checks.

5. **The resistance cascade is known** — published in peer-reviewed journals, confirmed in patient tissue. But the clinical guidelines do not require biomarker testing before each line of treatment. The system is not designed to identify non-responders early.

6. **The 340B program** allows hospitals to purchase drugs at steep discounts and bill at full price. The spread incentivizes expensive drugs over cheap generics. Patients pay the same copay regardless.

7. **Clinical trials are funded by the companies that profit from the drugs**. Trials that might show generic equivalence, or that might identify non-responders early, are not funded. The evidence base is shaped by financial incentives.

## CONCLUSION: THE SYSTEM IS WORKING AS DESIGNED

The Taxol story is not an anomaly. It is the template.

Public research discovers a molecule. A private company acquires exclusive rights through a non-competitive process. The company sets the price with no oversight. The drug generates billions in revenue. When the patent expires, the company reformulates the molecule and resets the price. The buy-and-bill system incentivizes the expensive reformulation over the cheap generic. Clinical trials are funded to support the expensive version. Biomarker testing that might identify non-responders is not funded. The resistance cascade is documented in the literature but not incorporated into clinical guidelines. Patients progress through multiple lines of treatment, each generating revenue, none addressing the molecular damage inflicted by the previous line.

**The verified numbers**:
- Public investment in Taxol discovery: Millions in NCI funding
- BMS revenue from Taxol by 2002: **>$9 billion**
- NIH royalties: **$35 million (0.39%)**
- Medicare overpayment: **>$500 million**
- Patient cost per course: **~$10,000**
- Generic paclitaxel cost per course today: **~$200–400**
- Abraxane cost per cycle today: **~$6,000–8,000**
- BMS acquisition of Celgene (Abraxane): **$74 billion**

This is not a conspiracy. It is a system. And the system is working exactly as designed — for everyone except the patient.
